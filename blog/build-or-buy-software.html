Build or buy? How to decide on new software

"Should we build this ourselves?" is one of the most expensive questions a business answers casually. A good decision takes an afternoon. A bad one can cost a year. Here is how we work through it with clients.

1. Name the problem, not the solution

"We need a CRM" is a solution. "We lose track of leads after the first call" is a problem. Write the problem in one sentence, and how you will know it is solved.

2. Price the status quo

What does the problem cost today, in hours, lost sales or mistakes? If the honest answer is "not much," the best option may be to wait.

3. Ask whether it sets you apart

Build what makes you different. Buy what makes you normal. Payroll, email and accounting rarely win you customers, so buy them. A pricing engine or customer experience that nobody else offers may be worth building.

4. Compare the full cost over three years

For buying: licences per user, setup, training, integrations and price increases. For building: design, development, hosting, security, support and the people needed to maintain it. Custom software is never finished, so budget for upkeep every year.

5. Compare time to value

An existing tool can often be running in weeks. A custom build usually takes months before anyone uses it. Ask what that delay costs you.

6. Check the exit

Can you export your data in a usable format? What happens if the vendor raises prices or shuts down? If you build, who can maintain the code if a developer leaves?

7. Consider the middle path

The answer is often both: buy a solid platform and build a small, custom layer on top of it. You get most of the speed of buying and keep what makes you different.

8. Write the decision down

Record the problem, the options, the numbers and why you chose. In a year, when someone asks "why did we do this?", you will have the answer, and a date to review it.

A quick rule of thumb

Buy when the problem is common and the tool is good enough. Build when it is central to how you win and nothing fits. Wait when the problem is cheap and the answer is unclear.
