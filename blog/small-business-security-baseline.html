A security baseline every small business needs

Most attacks on small businesses are not clever. They walk through doors that were left open: a reused password, a missing update, a backup nobody tested. Close these ten doors and you are ahead of most attackers.

1. Turn on multi-factor sign-in everywhere

Start with email, banking, cloud storage and your domain registrar. These accounts unlock everything else. Prefer passkeys or an authenticator app over text-message codes, which can be intercepted.

2. Use a password manager

Every account gets a long, unique password. A shared business password manager also means you can hand over or revoke access in one place when people join or leave.

3. Keep everything updated

Turn on automatic updates for operating systems, browsers and phones. For your website, plugins and servers, set a fixed monthly patch day and keep a list of what you run.

4. Back up, then test the restore

Keep at least one copy of important data somewhere attackers can't reach, such as an offline drive or storage that can't be changed or deleted for a set period. A backup only counts once you have restored from it.

5. Protect your email domain

Publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC records for your domain. They make it much harder for someone to send email pretending to be you, and they help your real emails land in the inbox. Gmail and Yahoo have required them for bulk senders since 2024.

6. Separate admin accounts

Nobody should browse the web or read email from an administrator account. Give staff the access their job needs, and nothing more.

7. Encrypt laptops and phones

Turn on full-disk encryption and a screen lock. A lost laptop is then a hardware cost, not a data breach.

8. Teach people to pause

Most breaches start with a message that creates urgency: an unpaid invoice, a changed bank account, a "quick favour" from the boss. Make it normal to check by phone before paying or sharing anything.

9. Offboard the same day

When someone leaves, remove their access to every system that day. Keep a checklist so nothing is forgotten, including shared passwords and devices.

10. Write a one-page incident plan

Who do you call? Who can shut things down? Where are the backups? Writing it down now saves hours when something goes wrong.

Where to start

If you only do three things this week, make them multi-factor sign-in, tested backups and automatic updates. They stop the most common attacks for the least effort. Then work down the list.
